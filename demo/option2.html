Women Who Sued the News

The EEOC Lawsuit Explosion Against News Organizations in the 1970s and Its Impact on Women in Media and Public Life

The 1970s marked a transformative era in the American workplace, particularly for women in the media. Spurred by the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and the subsequent rise of the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC), women across the United States began to challenge entrenched patterns of sex discrimination in employment.

This digital archive explores the explosion of lawsuits filed under the EEOC against news organizations during the 1970s, focusing on the legal and social impact these cases had on the status of women in the workplace, media, and public life. It serves as a foundation for the forthcoming book by Ashley Walter, PhD, and as a digital repository of oral histories and related materials.

Historical Context

Between 1970 and the early 1980s, women at nearly every major print news organization in the United States filed class-action sex discrimination lawsuits. These cases exposed systemic patterns of gender discrimination, challenged rigid job hierarchies, and forced industry-wide reforms in hiring, promotion, and pay practices.

The lawsuits were driven by courageous women who risked their careers and visionary attorneys who established legal precedents that extended far beyond the media industry.

Why Choose "Bold Justice"?

This design channels the activist spirit of the women who brought these lawsuits. Giant, confrontational typography and a deep-black background with red accents create immediate visual impact. The site doesn't whisper — it demands attention, just as the plaintiffs demanded equity.

Personality: Fearless, unapologetic, and modern. It positions the project as a living statement, not just a historical footnote. The aesthetic bridges the 1970s protest energy with contemporary social-justice design language.

Reminds people of: Campaign sites for social movements, Nike editorial features, The Intercept, or documentary film title sequences.

Best for audiences who respond to bold visual statements and want to feel the urgency of these stories. It's especially effective for reaching a younger, digitally-native audience and conveying that this history remains relevant today.
