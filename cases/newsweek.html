Newsweek (1970)

The first major class-action sex discrimination case against a national news magazine

← Back to Cases

Case Overview

Filed: 1970 | Settlement: 1972, 1973 | Legal Counsel: Eleanor Holmes Norton

The Newsweek suit was the first major class-action sex discrimination case against a national news magazine. Forty-six women, led by Lynn Povich, filed a complaint with the EEOC on March 16, 1970—the very day Newsweek published a cover story on the women's liberation movement. The timing was deliberate and ironic: management had assigned the story to an outside freelancer because they would not give it to a woman on staff.

The Discrimination

The case exposed a rigid gender hierarchy at Newsweek:

  • Men were writers and reporters
  • Women were confined to researcher positions
  • Women were systematically excluded from editorial and management roles
  • No women held senior editorial positions despite qualifications

This pattern reflected industry-wide practices at major news organizations where women's capabilities were limited by explicit gender-based job segregation.

Key Plaintiffs

Lynn Povich

Lead plaintiff and later author of "The Good Girls Revolt," Povich chronicled the organizing efforts and aftermath of the Newsweek suit. Her book provides invaluable insight into the personal risks and collective action that made the lawsuit possible.

Legal Representation

Eleanor Holmes Norton

Assistant legal director of the ACLU and later chair of the EEOC, Norton represented the Newsweek plaintiffs and became a national figure in civil rights law. Her expertise and commitment to social justice were instrumental in achieving the landmark settlement.

Outcome and Settlement

The case settled with a consent decree mandating:

  • Hiring and promotion goals for women
  • Commitments to expand opportunities for women in writing and reporting roles
  • Regular monitoring of progress

However, progress was slow. The initial settlement did not produce the expected changes, requiring a second round of complaints in 1972. This pattern of resistance and incremental change was common across cases at other news organizations.

Impact and Legacy

The Newsweek case set a precedent for subsequent lawsuits at other news organizations. It demonstrated that:

  • Women could organize collectively despite workplace intimidation
  • Gender discrimination in hiring and promotion was legally actionable
  • News organizations were not immune from civil rights enforcement
  • Public pressure and legal action could force institutional change

The case inspired similar organizing efforts at Reader's Digest, The New York Times, The Washington Post, and other major media outlets.
