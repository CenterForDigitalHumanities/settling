New York Times (1974)

Boylan et al. v. The New York Times - A watershed case for the industry

← Back to Cases

Case Overview

Filed: 1974 | Settlement: 1978 | Lead Plaintiff: Betsy Wade (Boylan) | Class Size: 545 women

Legal Counsel: Harriet Rabb, Columbia Law School's Employment Rights Project

Led by Betsy Wade (Boylan), the Times Women's Caucus filed a class-action suit after years of failed negotiations with management. The case, formally known as Boylan et al. v. The New York Times, documented stark disparities in hiring, promotion, and pay at what was considered the nation's most prestigious newspaper.

The Discrimination

The case documented systematic exclusion of women from positions of authority and influence:

  • In 1970, there were no women among the 21 masthead names
  • No women vice presidents in the organization
  • Only a handful of women in reporting or editorial roles
  • Women concentrated in lower-paying support and research positions
  • Significant wage gaps between men and women doing substantially equal work

Key Plaintiffs

Betsy Wade (Boylan)

The first woman copy editor at The New York Times and lead plaintiff in the case, Wade's oral history and memoirs document both the struggle and the incremental victories. Her leadership in organizing the Times Women's Caucus was instrumental in building the coalition necessary to file and sustain the lawsuit.

Legal Representation

Harriet S. Rabb

As director of Columbia Law School's Employment Rights Project, Rabb represented plaintiffs in multiple landmark cases including Newsweek, The New York Times, and Reader's Digest. Her clinical approach combined sophisticated legal strategy with coalition-building and public education, establishing precedents that extended beyond the media industry.

Outcome and Settlement

The 1978 settlement included:

  • Affirmative action plan with specific hiring and promotion goals
  • $350,000 in back pay distributed among class members
  • Monitoring mechanisms to track progress toward goals
  • Commitment to review salary structures and address pay disparities

Despite these gains, the average wage gap remained significant. The settlement represented both a victory and an acknowledgment of the limitations of legal remedies in addressing deeply embedded discrimination.

Impact and Legacy

The Times case was a watershed for both the newspaper and the industry at large. It demonstrated that even the most prestigious news organization was subject to civil rights enforcement and that women could successfully organize to challenge systemic discrimination.

The case contributed to:

  • Increased hiring and promotion of women into reporting and editorial positions
  • Women appearing in previously all-male departments
  • Greater attention to women's issues in editorial content
  • A model for organizing and litigation at other organizations
