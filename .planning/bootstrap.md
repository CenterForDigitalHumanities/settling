# Settling: Women Who Sued the News  
## The EEOC Lawsuit Explosion Against News Organizations in the 1970s and Its Impact on Women in Media and Public Life

---

## Introduction

The 1970s marked a transformative era in the American workplace, particularly for women in the media. Spurred by the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and the subsequent rise of the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC), women across the United States began to challenge entrenched patterns of sex discrimination in employment. Nowhere was this more visible—or more paradoxically underreported—than in the nation's newsrooms. This report explores the explosion of lawsuits filed under the EEOC against news organizations and other employers during the 1970s, focusing on the legal and social impact these cases had on the status of women in the workplace, media, and public life. It draws on landmark cases, oral histories, archival materials, and the broader context of the women's movement, serving as a foundation for the forthcoming book "Settling: Women Who Sued the News" by Ashley Walter, PhD, and as a blueprint for a digital archive of oral histories and related materials.

---

## The EEOC in the 1970s: Growth, Mandate, and Milestones

The EEOC, established by the Civil Rights Act of 1964, was initially tasked with investigating complaints of employment discrimination based on race, color, religion, sex, or national origin. By the 1970s, the agency's role had expanded dramatically, both in scope and authority. The number of discrimination charges handled by the EEOC grew from 8,854 in 1966 to over 20,000 by 1970, reflecting both increased public awareness and the persistence of discriminatory practices.

A pivotal moment came with the Equal Employment Opportunity Act of 1972, which granted the EEOC litigation authority, extended Title VII coverage to educational institutions, state and local governments, and the federal workforce, and lowered the threshold for employer coverage from 25 to 15 employees. These changes empowered the EEOC to pursue systemic discrimination cases and to intervene directly in industries—such as media and telecommunications—where patterns of sex-based exclusion were deeply entrenched.

The agency's priorities in the 1970s included reducing a massive backlog of cases, developing guidelines for employee selection procedures, and negotiating landmark consent decrees with major employers. Notably, the EEOC's actions against AT&T and the nation's steel producers set precedents for large-scale settlements involving back pay, affirmative action plans, and structural reforms.

## Legal Foundations: Landmark Supreme Court Decisions

The legal landscape of the 1970s was shaped by a series of Supreme Court decisions that clarified and expanded the reach of Title VII and related statutes. Several cases were particularly influential in the context of sex discrimination in the workplace:

- **Phillips v. Martin Marietta Corp. (1971):** The Court held that employers could not maintain one hiring policy for women with young children and another for men with similar family responsibilities, establishing the "sex-plus" theory of discrimination.
- **Griggs v. Duke Power Co. (1971):** The Court ruled that employment practices with a disparate impact on minorities or women must be justified by business necessity, shifting the focus from intent to effect.
- **Corning Glass Works v. Brennan (1974):** The Court clarified the allocation of proof in pay discrimination cases under the Equal Pay Act, requiring employers to justify pay differentials for substantially equal work.
- **General Electric Co. v. Gilbert (1976):** The Court controversially held that exclusion of pregnancy-related disabilities from health plans did not constitute sex discrimination, a decision later reversed by the Pregnancy Discrimination Act of 1978.
- **International Brotherhood of Teamsters v. United States (1977):** The Court established that in pattern-or-practice cases, once systemic discrimination is proven, all affected class members are presumed entitled to relief unless the employer proves otherwise.

These decisions provided the legal framework for class-action lawsuits against news organizations, enabling plaintiffs to challenge not only overt discrimination but also systemic practices that perpetuated gender inequality.

The cases often involved coalitions of women who organized in secret, gathered evidence, and sought legal representation—frequently from pioneering female attorneys and clinical law programs.

#### Case Summaries and Outcomes

**Newsweek (1970):**  
The Newsweek suit was the first major class-action sex discrimination case against a national news magazine. Forty-six women, led by Lynn Povich, filed a complaint with the EEOC on the very day Newsweek published a cover story on the women's liberation movement—ironically written by an outside freelancer because management would not assign it to a woman on staff. The suit exposed a rigid gender hierarchy: men were writers, women were researchers. The case settled with a consent decree mandating hiring and promotion goals, but progress was slow, requiring a second round of complaints in 1972.

**Reader’s Digest (1972):**  
Elaine Auerbach and seven other women, represented by Harriet Rabb and Columbia Law School's Employment Rights Project, filed suit after discovering that women were systematically excluded from senior editorial positions. The case revealed 235 of 316 job titles were sex-segregated, and women were concentrated in low-paying fulfillment roles. The 1977 settlement included $1.5 million in back pay and a requirement to implement diversity initiatives. Notably, the plaintiffs chose to share the settlement with all women at the company, including those who had opted out of the suit.

**New York Times (1974):**  
Led by Betsy Wade (Boylan), the Times Women's Caucus filed a class-action suit after years of failed negotiations with management. The case documented stark disparities: in 1970, there were no women among the 21 masthead names, no women vice presidents, and only a handful of women in reporting or editorial roles. The 1978 settlement included an affirmative action plan, hiring goals, and $350,000 in back pay, but the average wage gap remained significant. The case was a watershed for both the Times and the industry at large.

**Associated Press (AP):**  
Women at the AP, including Peggy Simpson and Virginia Sherlock, filed suit in the mid-1970s, resulting in a five-year affirmative action plan and pay equity measures. Like other organizations, the AP admitted no wrongdoing but agreed to substantial reforms.

**Other Cases:**  
Similar lawsuits were filed at the Washington Post, Detroit News, New Haven Journal-Courier, and Time Inc. publications. Each case followed a pattern of coalition-building, evidence-gathering, and protracted legal negotiations, often resulting in settlements that mandated hiring, promotion, and pay reforms.

## Key Legal Figures and Attorneys

The legal battles of the 1970s were shaped by a cadre of pioneering attorneys, many of them women, who brought both legal expertise and a deep commitment to social justice:

- **Eleanor Holmes Norton:** Assistant legal director of the ACLU and later chair of the EEOC, Norton represented the Newsweek plaintiffs and became a national figure in civil rights law.
- **Harriet S. Rabb:** As director of Columbia Law School's Employment Rights Project, Rabb represented plaintiffs in the Newsweek, New York Times, and Reader’s Digest cases. Her clinical approach combined legal strategy with coalition-building and public education.
- **Howard Rubin:** A student and later instructor at Columbia's Employment Rights Project, Rubin co-counseled several major cases, including Reader’s Digest and the New York Times.

These attorneys worked closely with plaintiffs, often facing resistance not only from employers but also from skeptical colleagues and judges. Their efforts established legal precedents and inspired a new generation of women lawyers.

## Prominent Plaintiffs and Oral Histories

The lawsuits were driven by courageous women who risked their careers and reputations to challenge discrimination. Their stories, preserved in oral histories and memoirs, provide invaluable insight into both the personal and collective dimensions of the movement.

### Notable Plaintiffs

- **Lynn Povich (Newsweek):** Lead plaintiff and author of "The Good Girls Revolt," Povich chronicled the organizing efforts and aftermath of the Newsweek suit.
- **Betsy Wade (New York Times):** The first woman copy editor at the Times and lead plaintiff in Boylan et al. v. The New York Times, Wade's oral history and memoirs document both the struggle and the incremental victories.
- **Elaine Auerbach (Reader’s Digest):** Auerbach's leadership in organizing the Digest suit and her subsequent memoir, "Dirty Linen," highlight the challenges of coalition-building and the emotional toll of litigation.
- **Peggy Simpson, Virginia Sherlock, Shirley Christian (AP):** These women played key roles in the AP suit and have contributed oral histories to various archives.

### Oral History Collections

Several major oral history projects have preserved the voices of these and other women:

- **Herstory: JAWS Oral History Project:** Thirty-three interviews with women journalists, including Betsy Wade, are available online, providing first-person accounts of newsroom culture and legal battles.
- **Washington Press Club Foundation Oral History Project:** Housed at Columbia University and the National Press Club Library, this collection includes interviews with Mary Lou Butcher (Detroit News), Eileen Shanahan (New York Times), and others.
- **Pennsylvania Newspaper Journalists Oral History Program:** This program at Penn State documents the experiences of journalists across the state, including those involved in discrimination suits.
- **Voices of Feminism Oral History Project (Smith College):** This collection includes interviews with labor, media, and grassroots activists, offering broader context for the women's movement.

These oral histories, along with personal records such as pay data, depositions, memos, and news clippings, form the backbone of a digital archive that can support both scholarly research and public education.

## Social and Workplace Impact

### Changes in Hiring, Promotion, and Pay

The lawsuits of the 1970s forced news organizations to confront and reform deeply embedded practices of gender discrimination. Key outcomes included:

- **Hiring and Promotion:** Consent decrees and settlements mandated the hiring and promotion of women into reporting, editorial, and management positions. At the New York Times, for example, the number of women in reporting roles increased significantly in the years following the settlement, and women began to appear in previously all-male departments.
- **Pay Equity:** Settlements often included back pay for affected women and required employers to review and adjust salary structures. Despite these gains, wage gaps persisted, reflecting both the limitations of legal remedies and the enduring power of informal networks.
- **Newsroom Culture:** The presence of women in newsrooms challenged the "symbolic annihilation" of women in media and contributed to shifts in editorial content, including more coverage of women's issues and perspectives.

### Media Coverage and Underreporting

Ironically, the news organizations that were defendants in these lawsuits often failed to report on their own legal troubles. As Ashley Walter notes, "these publications were not eager to report on their legal woes, so most of these lawsuits—including their diversity initiatives—were not covered by the mainstream press". This underreporting contributed to the relative obscurity of these landmark cases in public memory, despite their profound impact on the industry.

### Intersection with the Women's Movement and Labor Activism

The lawsuits were both a product of and a catalyst for the broader women's movement. Many plaintiffs were politicized through their reporting on feminism and labor issues, and they drew on strategies from both movements. The American Newspaper Guild, for example, provided crucial support in gathering salary data and funding litigation. The cases also intersected with efforts to address race discrimination, as seen in parallel suits at the New York Times and other organizations.

## Legal and Institutional Reforms

### Consent Decrees and Diversity Plans

Settlements in these cases often took the form of consent decrees—court-enforced agreements that required employers to implement specific reforms. Common elements included:

- **Numerical Goals:** Targets for hiring and promoting women and minorities into specific job categories.
- **Affirmative Action Plans:** Structured programs to recruit, train, and advance underrepresented groups.
- **Monitoring and Reporting:** Requirements for regular reporting to the court or an independent monitor on progress toward goals.

These mechanisms provided both accountability and a model for other industries.

### EEOC Enforcement and Broader Reforms

The EEOC's role in investigating, litigating, and publicizing these cases was critical. The agency's guidelines on employee selection procedures, pregnancy discrimination, and affirmative action set national standards that extended beyond the media industry. The passage of the Pregnancy Discrimination Act of 1978 and the expansion of EEOC authority under President Carter further strengthened legal protections for women workers.

## Archival Sources and Public Records

A robust digital archive of these cases can draw on a wide range of publicly available materials:

- **Court Filings:** Complaints, depositions, settlement agreements, and consent decrees from federal and state courts.
- **EEOC Records:** Investigation files, findings of fact, and conciliation agreements, accessible through the National Archives and the EEOC's FOIA process (with some restrictions).
- **Newspaper Archives:** Coverage (where it exists) in mainstream and alternative press, as well as internal newsletters and union publications.
- **Oral Histories and Memoirs:** Interviews, autobiographies, and personal papers of plaintiffs, attorneys, and journalists.
- **Photographs and Ephemera:** Images of newsroom life, protests, and key figures, many of which are available under open licenses from the Library of Congress and other repositories.

## Inspiration from Similar Digital Projects

Several digital projects provide models for the design and curation of a research-focused website:

- **Herstory Project:** A collaborative platform for women's, queer, and decolonial histories, featuring articles, podcasts, and a print journal. The site emphasizes accessibility, collaboration, and open submission policies.
- **Women's Suffrage and Media:** An online resource aggregating primary sources, scholarly articles, and teaching materials on the history of women's suffrage and media representation.
- **Penn State's Newspaper Journalists Oral History Program:** A joint project of Penn State's Department of Journalism and the Pennsylvania NewsMedia Foundation, this program preserves the life histories of leading journalists and makes interviews available for research and teaching.

These projects demonstrate the value of integrating oral histories, archival documents, and scholarly analysis in a user-friendly digital environment.

## Digital Archive Design: Metadata, Folder Structure, Content Types, and Citation Conventions

### Metadata Standards

Adopt the Dublin Core Metadata Element Set for describing digital objects, as recommended by the Collaborative Digitization Program and the Oral History in the Digital Age initiative. Key elements include:

- **Title**
- **Creator**
- **Subject**
- **Description**
- **Date (Original and Digital)**
- **Format**
- **Identifier (e.g., DOI, URL)**
- **Source**
- **Rights**
- **Contributing Institution**

For oral histories, include additional metadata such as interviewee and interviewer names, date and location of interview, summary, keywords, and rights information.

### Folder Structure

A recommended folder structure for the GitHub Pages repository:

```
/archive
    /oral-histories
        /transcripts
        /audio
        /metadata
    /photos
        /open-license
        /archival
    /court-documents
        /complaints
        /settlements
        /orders
    /press-clippings
    /personal-papers
    /teaching-materials
    /metadata
    /about
    /contact
    /style-guide
```

### Content Types

- **Markdown files** for narrative content, case summaries, and oral history transcripts.
- **PDFs** for scanned documents and court filings.
- **JPEG/PNG** for images and photographs.
- **Audio files** (MP3/WAV) for oral history recordings.
- **Metadata files** (XML/JSON) for structured data.

### Citation Conventions

- Use in-text citations in the format `[Author, Title, Date]` or as footnotes, linking to original sources where possible.
- For oral histories, include full metadata and a persistent identifier (e.g., DOI or stable URL).
- For images, provide source, date, and rights information.

## Website Style Guide

A clear and consistent style guide is essential for accessibility, usability, and visual identity. The following recommendations draw on best practices from leading digital style guides.

### Typography

- **Body Text:** Use a highly legible sans-serif font such as Open Sans, Lato, or Source Sans Pro at a minimum of 16px for body text.
- **Headings:** Use a bold, distinct font for headings (e.g., Montserrat, Roboto Slab). Maintain a clear hierarchy (H1, H2, H3).
- **Line Length:** Limit to 60–75 characters per line for readability.
- **Contrast:** Ensure a minimum contrast ratio of 4.5:1 between text and background.

### Color Palette

- **Primary Colors:** Deep blue (#1A237E), white (#FFFFFF), and charcoal gray (#212121).
- **Accent Colors:** Gold (#FFD600), teal (#009688), and soft lavender (#B39DDB).
- **Accessibility:** All color combinations should meet WCAG AA standards for contrast. Use tools like the Accessible Color Palette Generator to test combinations.

### Layout

- **Responsive Design:** Use a mobile-first, responsive layout with flexible grids.
- **Navigation:** Persistent top navigation bar with clear links to Archive, Oral Histories, Cases, About, and Contact.
- **Content Blocks:** Use cards or panels for case summaries, oral histories, and archival materials.
- **Whitespace:** Generous use of whitespace to reduce visual clutter.

### Accessibility

- **Alt Text:** All images must have descriptive alt text.
- **Keyboard Navigation:** Ensure all interactive elements are accessible via keyboard.
- **Screen Reader Support:** Use semantic HTML and ARIA labels as needed.
- **Forms:** Clearly labeled, single-column forms with visible error states.

### Imagery

- **Photographs:** Use high-quality, open-license images with captions.
- **Icons:** Use familiar, accessible icons for navigation and actions.
- **Data Visualizations:** Ensure sufficient contrast and provide text alternatives.

### Citation and Attribution

- **Footnotes:** Use superscript numbers or bracketed citations for references.
- **Image Credits:** Display source and rights information below each image.

## Conclusion

The explosion of EEOC lawsuits against news organizations in the 1970s was a watershed moment in the struggle for gender equality in the American workplace. These cases, driven by courageous women and visionary attorneys, transformed newsroom culture, advanced legal protections, and contributed to the broader women's movement. Yet, as the underreporting of these events by the media itself demonstrates, the history of women's fight for equality remains incomplete without the preservation and amplification of their voices.

The forthcoming book "Settling: Women Who Sued the News" and its companion digital archive aim to rectify this omission by providing a comprehensive, accessible, and richly documented account of these landmark cases. By integrating oral histories, archival materials, and scholarly analysis, the project will serve both as a resource for researchers and as a testament to the enduring impact of collective action.
